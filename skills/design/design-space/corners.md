# Corners

A **corner** is a concept pinned at the extremes of the axes and followed all the way through.
Generate four to six of them. They must be incompatible, and you present them flat.

The goal is not the answer. The goal is a visible space, so that the build is chosen against real
alternatives, not the first idea that cohered.

## What a corner is

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Name** | Two or three words, in the product's world. `The Ledger`, not `Option B`. The user argues with the name a week later. |
| **Thesis** | One line. What this corner believes that the others do not. |
| **Position** | Where it sits on every axis. A corner that sits mid-way on all of them is not a corner. |
| **Wins** | The one thing it does better than any other corner. |
| **Costs** | What it gives up. A corner with no cost is not followed through. |

A concept that the user hands you is a corner. Write it up in this shape next to the derived
corners, and hold it to the same bar. It arrives with evidence and their vocabulary, not with
immunity.

## Generating them

Do not brainstorm. Derive corners with moves that guarantee difference:

- **Pin one axis to each end.** This is the cheapest coverage: two corners that differ on one axis
  and follow the consequences everywhere else. Do this for the axis with the most at stake.
- **Take the extreme that nobody would pitch.** This is the corner that is obviously too much of
  something. It is rarely built, and it is almost always the one that moves the final answer.
- **Borrow a form from the audience's world.** Use a working system that they already read
  fluently, not a mood reference: a ledger, a patch bay, a field guide, a tide table, a rehearsal
  call sheet, a card catalogue. Carry its structure, its order, and its vocabulary, not its texture.
- **Change the primary object.** Most designs assume that the unit is the item. Try the collection,
  the event, the person, the change, or the time span as the thing that the interface is *of*. Of
  all the moves here, this one produces the most different corners.
- **Remove the obvious element.** No dashboard. No nav. No list. No search. Find what the category
  cannot imagine shipping without, and build the corner that does without it.
- **Follow the thesis past comfort.** Take the thesis literally, to the point where it starts to
  cost something, and design that.

If you can merge two corners without the loss of either idea, they were one corner. Merge them and
generate another.

## Sameness traps

If any of these traps fire, the corners are one idea in four hats:

- **The rename trap.** Four corners differ only in tone words. If the *structure* is the same, you
  generated one corner and four descriptions of it.
- **The Goldilocks trap.** Three corners, and the middle one is obviously correct. That is a
  recommendation with two props, not a space. Kill the props and generate two real rivals.
- **The lane trap.** All four corners sit in a lane that you already have priors for (dark + neon,
  cream + serif, terminal + mono). If someone can guess all four from the category alone, none of
  them are corners.
- **The feature trap.** The corners differ in what the product *does*, not in what it *is*. That is
  scoping, not design. Hand it back to the user as a product question and derive again.
- **The one-real-option trap.** Three corners that you would never build surround the one that you
  would. You must be ready to ship every corner on the list.

## Presenting

Use one message, in flat order. Give no ranking, no "my pick", and no leading adjectives on the
corners you like. The user's reaction to an unranked list is taste. Their reaction to a ranked list
is agreement.

Wait until the user asks which corner you would build. If they ask, answer honestly and say what
it costs. An evasion at that point reads as no view.

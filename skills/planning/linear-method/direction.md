# Direction

Shape *what* to build: initiatives, goals, prioritization, scope, specs, backlog.

Direction work is where the method spends its patience. Everything in `building.md` is biased
toward acting fast. That is safe only when the thing built fast is the right thing. So here, a
couple of weeks of thinking before you start is the default, not the failure mode.

## Set the product direction with initiatives

Source: [linear.app/method/product-direction](https://linear.app/method/product-direction)

Direction is the highest-value work in building a product and a company. An **initiative**
states a vision and how you intend to build toward it. The set of initiatives becomes a product
timeline that execution reads from.

- Write initiatives so that a team that works independently can make a call without asking.
- Keep them visible to the whole company. Visible initiatives show people that their work
  matters. Transparency makes the direction trusted, not only announced.
- Reserve capacity against them for unexpected work. An initiative that consumes 100% of a team
  is a plan that breaks on the first incident.

**Done when:** each planned project sits under a named initiative, and each initiative states the
vision and the intended path, not only a theme.

## Set useful goals

Source: [linear.app/method/set-useful-goals](https://linear.app/method/set-useful-goals)

The usual objection to goals at an early stage is that you lack the data for a defensible
number. That is true, but it is not a reason to skip the goal. Here the goal creates direction
and a measurable notion of forward. It does not need to be forecast-accurate.

- With no user data yet, a goal is useful if it **propels you forward in some measurable way**.
  It does not need evidence that you do not have.
- **Work backwards.** 10 users requires 1 user, which requires a product that is discoverable and
  usable. Name the step immediately before the target, and make that the goal.
- Scale in visible rungs: 10 users -> 100 users -> $1,000 MRR. Successful startups start with
  something small, figure it out, then scale.

**Done when:** the goal names a number and a date, and it names the rung immediately before it.

## Prioritize enablers and blockers

Source: [linear.app/method/prioritize-enablers-and-blockers](https://linear.app/method/prioritize-enablers-and-blockers)

Resources and time are finite, so each priority call must be explainable. Classify each candidate:

- **Enabler**: adds value that the product does not have yet.
- **Blocker**: friction or a gap that prevents people from using what is already there.

Then answer three questions, in order:

1. **Impact**: if this succeeds, does it move a higher-level goal?
2. **Timeliness**: must this be done now, or can it be done later without penalty?
3. **Compounding**: does building it now create advantage, or complexity and support cost?

Not all friction is a blocker. Separate *prevents use* from *improves the experience*.

Linear launched with Google Login only and deferred email and other methods. Velocity elsewhere
was worth more than complete authentication.

**Done when:** each prioritized item carries an enabler or blocker label and a now-or-later answer,
and each deprioritized item has a stated reason.

## Scope projects down

Source: [linear.app/method/scope-projects](https://linear.app/method/scope-projects)

The size limit is not an estimation convention. It is a forcing function. A three-week ceiling
makes the team cut to the most important feature set, because there is no room for more.
If you loosen the ceiling, that pressure disappears.

- A project **must complete in 1-3 weeks with 1-3 people**. Smaller work takes hours or a day.
- Short timelines force you to pick the most important feature set. That is the mechanism, not a
  side effect.
- Small teams move faster and cut management and communication overhead.
- Early on, you cannot predict whether a project will have impact, so large bets are expensive
  guesses. Continuous shipping gives quick feedback loops with customers instead.
- **If you cannot reduce the scope, break it into stages.** Ship an early version to your team and
  private beta users in the first week. Collect feedback immediately. Fix in the weeks after.

Linear's Cycles and Projects each took about two weeks to design and build in MVP form.

**Done when:** no project exceeds 3 weeks or 3 people, or it is explicitly staged into ones that do
not. The first stage ships to internal or beta users.

## Specs, owners, and the backlog

- **Each project has one named owner.** The owner writes the brief and is responsible for delivery.
- **Write project specs, and keep them short.** People read short specs and skim long ones. The
  owner writes the spec and gathers feedback until the right approach is clear. Expect a couple of
  weeks of thinking before building starts.
- **Keep the backlog manageable.** A focused backlog makes cycle planning fast. Prune it. An item
  that nobody will pull is noise with a due date.
- **Understand your users.** Keep collected feedback as a research library to develop against,
  not as a queue to service.

**Done when:** each project has one owner and a spec short enough to read in one sitting, and the
backlog contains only work that someone can plausibly pull into a cycle.
